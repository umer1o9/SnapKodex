Multi-tenant SaaS: pool, bridge or silo?

The tenancy model shapes cost, isolation and operations for years. A practical guide to choosing it and to proving isolation works.

By Snap Kodex Engineering·5 Nov 2026·8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Pooled tenancy is cheapest and demands database-level isolation; siloed is simpler to isolate and costlier to run.
  • Choose from data sensitivity, customer size and budget, not from habit.
  • Write tests that deliberately try to read another tenant's data and run them on every build.

In this article
  1. The three models
  2. How to choose
  3. Enforce isolation in layers
  4. Plan the promotion path
  5. Offboarding

If you build software that many customers use, you will choose a tenancy model whether you intend to or not. Making the choice on purpose is cheaper than discovering it during a security review.

The three models

Pool

All tenants share the same tables, separated by a tenant identifier on every row. It is the cheapest to run and the easiest to onboard, because a new tenant is a new row rather than new infrastructure. The risk is that one missing filter leaks data between customers, and that a heavy tenant can slow the others.

Silo

Each tenant gets its own database, or its own full stack. Isolation is simple to explain and to audit, and tenant-specific backup, residency and customisation are easy. The cost is operational: many databases to migrate, monitor and pay for.

Bridge

A mix. Small tenants share a pool; large or regulated tenants are promoted to dedicated resources. It is a common destination for growing products.

How to choose

  • Data sensitivity and regulation. Health, finance and public-sector customers often require stronger isolation or specific regions.
  • Customer size and count. Thousands of small customers favour pooling; a few large ones may justify silos.
  • Customisation. Heavy per-tenant schema changes are painful in a pool.
  • Team and budget. A small team operating hundreds of databases will struggle.

Enforce isolation in layers

  1. Carry tenant identity from login to the query. The tenant is part of the authenticated context, not a parameter the client supplies.
  2. Enforce in the database. Row-level security policies mean a forgotten filter in application code cannot leak data.
  3. Constrain noisy neighbours. Per-tenant rate limits, quotas and queue isolation protect everyone from one heavy user.
  4. Test the boundary. Write automated tests that authenticate as one tenant and try to read, write and enumerate another's data. Run them in CI.

Plan the promotion path

Even in a pooled design, make it possible to move one tenant to dedicated storage later. Avoid cross-tenant joins, keep identifiers globally unique and keep tenant data exportable. That turns a future enterprise requirement into a project rather than a rewrite.

Offboarding

Decide at the start how a tenant's data is exported and deleted. It is much easier to design in than to retrofit when your first customer asks to leave.
